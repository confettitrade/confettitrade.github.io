Blockfront: The Graph for Hyperliquid

November 2026

Blockfront is The Graph for Hyperliquid: a signed RPC on nodes we run, plus a market for data recipes we call transformers. The Graph proved an open data layer can be worth billions, then wrecked its own token by printing it instead of charging customers. Hyperliquid is where the data movers are, and we think perp DEXs will be the customers. Our version is already live with plans priced in USDC, and the design behind it was waiting for this chain.

01

The Graph proved the need

The Graph went live on Ethereum on 17 December 2020 with a simple idea. Apps should read chain data from an open network, not from one company’s servers. The Graph says it has since served over 1.27 trillion queries to more than 75,000 projects on 60+ networks.

That is demand at scale, and it set a standard. The subgraph became a common format for chain data, and hosts like Goldsky and Ormi sell Graph-compatible subgraphs. Our transformers use the subgraph as their design reference.

We are not affiliated with The Graph. We use its name because it proved the need.

The problems it had

Most real demand never touched the paid network. In mid-2022 the free hosted service, run by one company, carried over 25,000 subgraphs against 392 on the network. It was meant to close in early 2023 and ran until 12 June 2024.

Curation, the system meant to point Indexers at good subgraphs, was gamed within weeks of launch. Researchers counted 626 buy-and-sell trades by 201 curators on 63 subgraphs, each within two minutes of deployment. Core developers later wrote that signal does not reliably predict query demand.

The problems it still has

The rewards did not follow the work. By The Graph’s own figures, 15.2% of 2025 indexing rewards went to Indexers adding effectively no value. Another 11.8% went to Indexers with very low uptime or few query-producing subgraphs.

Much of the traffic was not decentralized either. By The Graph’s own account, a large share of developer queries was served by a centralized Upgrade Indexer, not the network. Moving that traffic to the network only began on 8 October 2026.

Revenue stayed thin. In Q4 2025 the network took $98.7K in query payments, while query volume fell 8.9% to 4.97 billion.

Its own Foundation says the previous mandate did not deliver the growth the protocol needs. At least one chain has left: Kaia ended its support on 31 August 2026.

And on Hyperliquid, The Graph is barely there. Its registry lists no subgraph support for HyperEVM and has no entry for HyperCore at all. It reaches HyperEVM only through Substreams, run by Pinax.

To be fair, the network is not dead. It still serves about 5 billion queries a quarter, and it shipped real fixes: a free tier, Substreams and Horizon. Since 25 August 2026, an eligibility oracle pays only Indexers that serve queries.

The network survived. The token is another story.

02

The Graph as an investment

The Graph was one of the best investments of its cycle for anyone who got in early. Its backers put in $7.5 million for about a third of the supply, about $0.002 a token on The Graph’s own numbers. At the February 2021 peak of $2.84 that stake was worth about $9.7 billion on paper, and it is still worth about $59 million today. Public-sale buyers at $0.03 saw 95x at the peak. Everyone who came later and held has lost: GRT trades at $0.0173 on 14 November 2026, 99.4% below that peak.

03

Tokenomics: what The Graph got wrong

The Graph’s tokenomics wrecked the token and its long-term holders. Yet they still made early backers insane money. Both outcomes come from the same design.

The main mistake: too much to VCs

Backers put in $7.5 million for about 34% of the 10 billion genesis supply. On The Graph’s own numbers that is about $0.002 a token, roughly a fourteenth of the $0.03 the public paid. The public got 4%, and Indexers and community members bought another 2%.

About 85% of genesis supply went to the team, the backers, the Foundation and Edge & Node. It then unlocked into a market with almost no revenue. Circulating supply went from 1.25 billion to 8.72 billion GRT in two years.

The result: GRT fell 91% in 2022, against 64% for bitcoin and 67.5% for ether.

Hand in hand: printing tokens instead of charging customers

The network was paid for with new GRT, not customer money. In 2025 users paid about $457,000 for subgraph queries, while the protocol issued about $27.5 million of new GRT to Indexers. From 2023 to 2025 it was about $1.2 million paid by users against about $132 million in new tokens.

The price kept revenue tiny: $2 per 100,000 queries, after 100,000 free a month. Even its record quarter, 6.49 billion queries in Q2 2025, brought in about $129,000 against $6.0 million of rewards. The token never depended on usage.

These are not two mistakes. They are one. The token was built for the people who funded it, not the people who run it.

In our view, the VCs served their interests and not the operators’. The backers are the only early group still up, about 7.8x as a group on The Graph’s numbers. Public-sale buyers who held sit at about 0.58x, and operators were paid in printed tokens from a token now 99% below its peak.

A public good, paid for by holders

At this point The Graph is basically a public good for web3 builders. Builders get cheap or free queries, and new tokens cover the rest. Holders pay through dilution: total supply has grown from 10 billion to about 11.6 billion GRT.

Yet it still made people insane money

Before the token sank, it soared. Public-sale buyers saw 95x at the February 2021 peak, and the backers’ stake was worth about $9.7 billion on paper. The design rewarded getting in early, as the investment section above shows.

What doing it right looked like

The demand was real: 1.27 trillion queries and 75,000 projects. In our view, with tokenomics that charged customers, The Graph could be profiting hugely off query as a service. Instead, its own Foundation says chain-integration revenue is largely captured outside the protocol.

Hyperliquid shows the other path. Its docs say it took no VC funding, and DefiLlama puts what its users pay at about $1.1 billion a year at the current rate. Charge customers, and the network pays for itself.

What we learned

  • Customers pay real money. Plans are priced in USDC: free, then 29, 149 and 399 USDC per 30 days, live since 3 November. Customers never need our token to use Blockfront.
  • Operators are paid from revenue, not printing. 30% of every data payment is set aside for the operators who serve it. Today those operators are our own two nodes.
  • No inflation. Our token has a fixed supply of 1 billion, minted once, with no mint function.
  • No token sold. The Graph handed about a third of its supply to backers. We have not sold a single token, to a fund or to anyone.
  • No first-mover games. Curation is plain pro-rata with no bonding curve, and no contract charges an entry tax. It stays switched off until it is ready.
  • Burn only from real revenue. A set share of data payments, 10% today, is held for buyback and burn. The contract never lets it fall below 1%.

Those lessons only pay off on the right chain.

04

Hyperliquid is the chain for data

The Graph grew up on Ethereum. Ethereum is built for settlement, and settlement is a different job from data.

What Ethereum chose

Ethereum made deliberate trade-offs, and they are good ones for a settlement layer. Blocks come every 12 seconds, and finality takes about 15 minutes, a pace chosen so home computers can validate. Block size is capped so modest nodes can keep up.

Those same choices make it a hard chain for data. Since the Merge, about 15,500 blocks have been dropped in short reorgs, about 10 a day, and every indexer must roll them back. Nodes can now shed history too: pre-Merge data since July 2025, and rollup blobs after about 18 days.

The activity is scattered as well. ETHBEAT (formerly L2BEAT) lists 90 Ethereum scaling projects, and Uniswap v3 alone runs on 19 networks. Most market data, from oracle prices to order books, is made off Ethereum’s main chain.

dYdX learned this in 2022. It said no L1 or L2 it could build on came close to what its order book needs. So it moved to its own chain.

What Hyperliquid chose

Hyperliquid made the opposite trade. Every order, cancel, trade and liquidation runs on chain with one-block finality, at about 14 blocks a second. Validators post oracle prices as on-chain transactions about every 3 seconds, and funding settles every hour.

Positions, balances and margin are chain state, not something rebuilt from token logs. HyperEVM shares that state, so a contract can read the exact mark and oracle prices the exchange uses. With one-block finality there are no reorgs to unwind, so the effort goes into checking answers instead.

Where the data movers are

This is the heart of it: Hyperliquid is where the data movers are. By our rough sample of recent blocks, HyperCore carries more than 20,000 transactions a second, almost all orders and cancels. Ethereum’s busiest day on record was 3.6 million transactions; HyperCore moves that many in under three minutes.

The money follows the data. On 14 November, Hyperliquid’s own API showed about $12 billion in 24-hour volume. DefiLlama put it at about 42% of tracked perp-DEX volume and 55% of open interest.

CoinGecko ranks Hyperliquid third by open interest among all derivatives venues, centralized exchanges included. Only Binance and OKX are ahead.

Yet that data is under-served. Hyperliquid’s one official EVM RPC allows 100 requests a minute per IP and serves no historical state. Its API returns only a user’s 10,000 most recent fills.

Running your own node takes 128 GB of RAM and writes about 100 GB of logs a day. Most teams will not do that.

Hyperliquid has trade-offs of its own: 27 active validators and heavy nodes. We do not call it fully decentralized. We call it the chain where the data is.

So here is our thesis. The Graph was built on a settlement chain and spread across 60+ networks, while Hyperliquid is the blockchain for data. That is why we think the Graph for Hyperliquid is a better investment than The Graph was: a thesis, not a promise of returns.

Why now

This is arguably a better time. When The Graph launched, Ethereum still ran on proof of work and perp DEXs barely registered. Castle Labs counts perp DEXs at 0.11% of all perps volume as late as January 2023, and 12.33% in September 2026.

HyperEVM went live in February 2025, and HIP-3 builder-deployed perps in October 2025. Data vendors are still filling the HyperCore gap this year, and most need a paid plan, a sales call or a preview.

Then there is Binance. On 29 October it lost about $6.3 billion, the largest crypto theft on record. Attackers got in through a fake job interview that compromised an engineer’s computer, and the FBI blamed North Korea.

Binance’s accountant, Mazars, stopped its proof-of-reserves work in December 2022. Its proof of reserves after the hack is self-prepared, and the Journal of Accountancy says it is not an audit. In the first nine and a half hours after withdrawals reopened, it recorded about $3.6 billion in net outflows.

Binance says it covered the $6.3 billion itself. Its user protection fund, SAFU, holds about $1 billion. With no independent audit, nobody outside can check where the rest came from.

This hack is just proof of why decentralization is key. One company’s engineer was the way in, and one company holds its customers’ coins. Data has the same weak point, so Blockfront is designed for many operators and signed answers.

We think Hyperliquid is poised to become the decentralized version of Binance. On a centralized exchange, your coins sit in their wallets and you hold an IOU. On Hyperliquid, you trade from your own wallet and every order and fill is public.

Not your keys, not your crypto.

05

Perp DEXs are the customers

So who pays? Realistically, perp DEXs will be the main customers for a protocol like this. On 14 November, perps were about 98% of Hyperliquid’s 24-hour volume, by our sums of its API.

Builder-deployed HIP-3 markets were about a third of that. Ten HIP-3 perp DEXs are registered, four with live markets, and each deployer stakes 500,000 HYPE to launch one.

Perp businesses run on data: PnL, funding history, liquidation prices, leaderboards and points. Each one is a table someone has to build, keep current and serve fast. Hyperliquid’s own leaderboard is a 39 MB JSON file on S3, outside its documented API.

And the money is there. Front ends earn a builder’s cut on the trades they route. By late May 2026, Phantom had earned about $20.6 million and MetaMask about $6.5 million.

These are businesses with budgets and a daily need for clean data. Most will not run 128 GB nodes to get it. We think they will pay for it, the way they pay for everything else they cannot build.

Our first user is Confetti, our own perp DEX. It ran its trading site and its trading bots through Blockfront from 4 November. Today’s transformers read HyperEVM; HIP-3 markets live in HyperCore, and HyperCore-native transformers come next.

06

Transformers for Hyperliquid

What we sell them is the transformer.

A transformer is a data recipe. It reads raw chain events and turns them into clean tables you can query, like every swap on a DEX. Every answer comes signed by the machine that served it, naming the block it describes.

How we make them

Our command-line tool does it in five steps: scaffold, build, record real blocks, test, and publish with a price. Before listing, we check the output against other sources, such as the official RPC. Listed transformers are open source under the MIT license.

How we sell them

Buyers pay in USDC from their own wallet. A query costs 1 unit plus 1 per row returned, priced per 1,000 units, and costs the same on every operator.

Each payment splits 50% to the publisher, 30% to operators by the answers they signed, 10% to curators and 10% to a burn reserve. Until curation opens, the curators’ share goes to the publisher. The publisher of a good recipe earns from every query it serves.

Selling to Hyperliquid itself

The buyer we want most is Hyperliquid itself. It already does data work in house: its validators publish oracle prices every 3 seconds. Its GitHub hosts outside-built stats code and an order-book server.

We found no record of it paying an outside data vendor. So this is a proposal, not a deal.

Simply put, it would look like this:

  1. Hyperliquid tells us the data it wants.
  2. We write the transformer, and our nodes run it and sign every answer.
  3. Hyperliquid’s team queries one endpoint and checks the signatures with our open verify library.

Hyperliquid asks once, runs no new servers, and can check every answer it gets back.

What Hyperliquid would get:

  • Deeper history. Full tables, instead of an API capped at a user’s 10,000 most recent fills.
  • Answers it can check. Every answer signed, naming the block it came from.
  • No new servers. No 128 GB nodes to run and no 100 GB of daily logs to parse.
  • Relief for its own endpoints. Builders query us instead of its one official RPC, capped at 100 requests a minute per IP.
  • A second pair of eyes. Our first transformer for it already found an action id Hyperliquid’s docs do not list.

The first one is already built

It decodes every action HyperEVM contracts send to HyperCore. Over 300,000 HyperEVM blocks it found 309,290 actions from 6,499 senders, 93% of them USDC deposits. Eleven used action id 14, which Hyperliquid’s docs still do not list.

It is parity-checked and waiting to be listed. Once outside operators can join, Hyperliquid could even run its own operator for it.

It is the first transformer we built for Hyperliquid. It will not be the last.

07

Blockfront: everything The Graph was, and more

The design came first. It was waiting for the right chain.

The original Blockfront papers describe nodes that run data recipes from a curated store, with a two-thirds quorum signing each result. Hyperliquid is that chain. So we built it there.

What is live today

  • One RPC, our own nodes. rpc.blockfront.network sits in front of our two Hyperliquid nodes, in New Jersey and Tokyo, with public RPCs as a fallback.
  • Real traffic. 2,293,463 requests in the 24 hours to 04:40 UTC on 14 November, with 0 errors in the last 5 minutes.
  • Signed answers. Every RPC answer is signed with the block number and hash it describes.
  • Proofs that work. Cross-checks between our two nodes caught a real bug on day one: 111 of 10,833 blocks disagreed. We fixed it, and they agree today.
  • Confetti ran on it. Confetti, our own perp DEX, ran its trading site and its trading bots through Blockfront from 4 November. In the first 8 minutes after the bots switched, 17 trades confirmed and none failed.
  • Revenue rails. Paid plans in USDC since 3 November, and a live transformer market with the split above. Anyone can try the RPC free at 5 requests a second.

Today we run every operator ourselves. That is the starting line, not the design.
